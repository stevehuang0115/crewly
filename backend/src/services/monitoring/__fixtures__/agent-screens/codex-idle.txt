
  Tides shape life as surely as they shape shorelines. In the intertidal zone, crabs, mussels, sea stars, and seaweeds
  endure alternating hours of immersion and exposure. Their feeding, movement, and reproduction often follow the tide’s
  rhythm. Birds arrive on exposed flats to search for food, then retreat as the water returns. People organize fishing
  trips, ferry crossings, beach walks, and port operations around the same cycle. Tidal currents carry sediment, open
  channels, and nourish wetlands. Over years, these repeated movements help build and reshape the places where land and
  sea meet.

  Understanding tides matters beyond curiosity. Accurate forecasts help mariners avoid grounding, help communities plan
  for flooding, and help scientists track changes in coastal ecosystems. Rising global sea levels mean that a high tide
  can reach farther inland than it once did, even without a storm. In some cities, flooding now arrives when ordinary
  tides combine with higher background water levels. Tides remind us that the coast is never fixed, even on a calm day.
  Each shoreline belongs to a moving planetary system, and each daily change connects a local view to the Moon, the Sun,
  and Earth’s rotation.
